What is a retailer deduction?

For CPG brands selling through distributors and retailers — from the first deduction on your remittance through classification, dispute, and prevention.

A retailer deduction is money withheld from a supplier's invoice payment. Shortage claims, compliance fees, chargebacks, pricing adjustments, and promotional pass-throughs — every one of them reduces the cash a CPG brand collects. This guide covers what they are, why they happen, how much they cost, and how to manage them.

Direct answer

Money withheld from your invoice. Not a payment-card chargeback.

A retailer deduction — also called a chargeback, shortage claim, or vendor deduction — is money withheld from a supplier's invoice payment by a retailer or distributor. These are not payment-card chargebacks. They are line-item reductions applied by KeHE, UNFI, Walmart, Target, Kroger, and others against the amounts they owe suppliers.

CPG brands typically lose 5-15% of gross sales to these deductions. Most are contractual — charges that follow agreed terms like trade spend, slotting fees, and MCB processing. Some are not. The difference between accepting a valid charge and recovering an invalid one is evidence, classification, and speed.

The terms “deduction,” “chargeback,” “shortage claim,” and “vendor deduction” are used interchangeably across the industry. Distributors and retailers each use their own terminology, but the mechanics are the same: you invoiced $X, you received $X minus something, and the difference is a deduction.

Eight categories

Types of retailer deductions

Every deduction on a CPG brand's remittance falls into one of these categories. Understanding the type is the first step to knowing whether it's contractual or worth disputing.

Shortage claims

Often disputable

Quantity received at the DC is less than quantity invoiced. The distributor deducts the difference.

Disputable when the BOL, POD, or packing list contradicts the DC count. Double-deductions (IA + KC on the same PO) have a historically high win rate.

Common at

KeHE, UNFI, Walmart, Target, Kroger

Compliance fees

Sometimes disputable

Violations of routing, labeling, EDI, appointment, or packaging requirements trigger per-occurrence or percentage-based fees.

Disputable when the compliance event was caused by the distributor (rescheduled appointment, DC-side delay) rather than the supplier.

Common at

KeHE, UNFI, Walmart, Target

Promotional chargebacks

Rarely disputable

Trade spend deductions for slotting, placement, introductory allowances, and promotional programs.

Usually contractual — the terms were agreed in advance. Disputable only when the rate, duration, or scope does not match the signed agreement.

Common at

KeHE, UNFI, all major retailers

Pricing adjustments

Sometimes disputable

The invoiced price does not match the price the distributor has on file. The difference is deducted.

Disputable when the invoice reflects the current agreed price and the distributor has not updated their system. Requires the signed deal sheet or price confirmation.

Common at

KeHE, UNFI, Walmart, Kroger

Spoilage and damage

Mixed

Product arrived damaged, expired, or within the minimum-remaining-shelf-life window. A fixed-rate allowance or per-incident charge is applied.

Fixed-rate allowances (like KeHE CS) are usually contractual. Event-driven damage claims are disputable when photos, POD notations, or temperature logs show the product was intact at handoff.

Common at

KeHE (CS, G, Q codes), UNFI

Service level fines

Often disputable

Fill rate falls below the distributor threshold (commonly 95%). A percentage-based fee is assessed on the shortage.

Disputable when the fill-rate calculation is based on a DC recount rather than the supplier shipment. A signed BOL showing full quantity is decisive evidence.

Common at

KeHE (SLF code), UNFI

Minimum order charges

Check program status

Order volume falls below the distributor minimum. A processing fee is applied (e.g., KeHE MCB at 8%).

KeHE MCB is waived for suppliers enrolled in the AAP (Administrative Allowance Program). Verify enrollment before disputing.

Common at

KeHE (MCB code)

Freight and logistics fees

Rarely disputable

Carrier charges, fuel surcharges, lumper fees, and inbound receiving charges passed through to the supplier.

Most freight fees are contractual passthroughs. Disputable when the fee was caused by a distributor-side issue (DC not ready at appointment, forced detention).

Common at

KeHE (FF prefix), UNFI

Root causes

Why deductions happen — and why they compound

Deductions are not random. They follow predictable patterns driven by structural complexity in CPG wholesale operations. Understanding the root causes is the first step toward prevention.

01

Every distributor uses different codes, portals, and rules

KeHE uses 111 prefix codes. UNFI has its own taxonomy. Walmart, Target, and Kroger each have separate deduction systems. A single CPG brand selling through three distributors manages three completely different deduction environments with no shared interface.

02

No single source of truth across systems

The evidence needed to understand a deduction lives across EDI records, email, the WMS, carrier tracking, retailer portals, and spreadsheets. No one system holds the complete picture. By the time a deduction posts, the people and evidence closest to the operational event have often moved on to newer work.

03

Evidence is scattered and time-decays

A BOL from three months ago, an appointment confirmation in an email thread, a packing list in a shared drive, and a carrier scan in a portal. The evidence exists, but assembling it takes hours per deduction. Many teams give up on small-dollar deductions because the research cost exceeds the recovery value.

04

Dispute windows are strict and unforgiving

KeHE requires disputes filed through K-Solve within 180 days. UNFI timelines vary by category but are generally shorter. Missing a deadline means the deduction is permanent — even if the evidence clearly shows the charge was wrong.

05

The volume is relentless

A CPG brand with $5M in annual wholesale revenue might see 400+ deduction lines per year. At $10M, it can be 1,000+. Each one needs to be classified, investigated, and either accepted or disputed — and the team managing them rarely grows with the volume.

The critical distinction

Contractual vs. disputable: classify before you dispute

The most expensive mistake in deduction management is disputing everything. Most deduction dollars are contractual — they follow agreed terms and should be verified, not contested. Disputing contractual charges wastes time, erodes distributor credibility, and buries the recoverable items in noise.

88%

Contractual passthrough

In one verified audit, $140,914 of $151,671 in total deductions were contractual. Trade spend, promotional allowances, MCB processing fees, and spoilage deposits that follow the signed P&P Terms Form. These should be verified for accuracy, not disputed.

Examples

  • CS: Customer Spoilage Allowance (fixed rate)
  • MCB: MCB Processing Fee (8% of promo allowance)
  • CN: Weekly pass-through charges
  • P/IP/NP: Placement and introductory allowances

12%

Potentially disputable

The remaining $10,757 contained items where the charge appeared inconsistent with the PO, shipment record, or applicable rule. Of those, $5,940 was estimated recoverable with proper documentation. This is where deduction management earns its return.

Examples

  • SLF: Service Level Fine (DC miscount, not a real shortage)
  • IA: Invoice Adjustment (duplicate charge on same PO)
  • FF: Freight fee (DC-caused detention, not supplier)
  • KC: Post-audit shortage (double-deduction with IA)

Real data from a verified deduction audit. Results vary by distributor, volume, trade programs, and compliance track record.

The hidden cost

What happens when deductions go unclassified

When a CPG brand writes off deductions without classifying them, three things compound silently:

Recoverable dollars disappear

The disputable 12% gets absorbed with the contractual 88%. At $5M in annual revenue with a 10% deduction rate, that is $25,000-$75,000 per year in recoverable value that never gets contested.

Root causes stay invisible

Without classification, recurring operational failures — a DC that consistently miscounts, a carrier that misses appointment windows, a labeling issue at a specific warehouse — remain hidden in the noise.

Dispute windows close permanently

KeHE gives 180 days. UNFI can be shorter. Every day without classification is a day closer to a deadline that makes the deduction permanent, regardless of the evidence.

Frequently asked

Common questions about retailer deductions

What is the difference between a retailer deduction and a payment-card chargeback?+

They share the word "chargeback" but are completely different. A payment-card chargeback is a consumer reversing a credit card transaction through their bank. A retailer deduction is a distributor or retailer withholding money from a supplier's invoice payment — shortage claims, compliance fees, promotional chargebacks, and pricing adjustments. They are B2B line-item reductions, not consumer disputes. EasyOps manages retailer deductions, not payment-card chargebacks.

What percentage of deductions are actually disputable?+

In one verified audit, 88% of deduction dollars ($140,914 of $151,671) were contractual passthrough — charges that follow agreed terms and should not be disputed. The remaining 12% contained $5,940 in estimated recoverable value. The exact split depends on your trade programs, compliance track record, and distributor mix, but the pattern is consistent: most deduction dollars are contractual. The audit's job is to separate the two so effort goes where it matters.

How long do I have to dispute a deduction?+

It depends on the distributor. KeHE requires disputes filed through K-Solve within 180 days of the deduction date. UNFI timelines vary by deduction category and are generally shorter. Walmart, Target, and Kroger each have their own deadlines and submission processes. Missing any deadline makes the deduction permanent, regardless of the evidence.

What evidence do I need to dispute a deduction?+

The evidence depends on the cause, not the deduction code. For shortages: the BOL, proof of delivery, and packing list. For compliance violations: appointment records, carrier tracking, and the communication showing who caused the issue. For pricing: the signed deal sheet and the PO. For every type: the evidence must be specific to the PO and deduction date, not a generic company document. See the complete evidence checklist for details by cause.

Can deduction management be automated?+

Classification and evidence assembly can be automated. EasyOps classifies each deduction line as contractual or disputable using validated rules and attaches the BOL, ASN, appointment record, and rule citation automatically. For KeHE and UNFI portals, automated dispute submission is available. The judgment calls — whether to escalate, accept, or investigate further — still require a human decision informed by operational context.

How much do CPG brands lose to deductions?+

CPG brands typically lose 5-15% of gross sales to retailer and distributor deductions. The exact rate depends on product categories, trade programs, compliance performance, and distributor mix. Not all of this is recoverable — most deduction dollars are contractual passthrough. The recoverable portion is approximately 5-15% of total deductions based on a single verified audit, meaning 0.5-2% of gross sales is potentially left on the table when deductions go unclassified.

Evidence boundary: This guide is a practical reference, not a substitute for current distributor documentation or a determination that any specific deduction is recoverable. Always verify current terms, evidence, and deadlines directly with each distributor.
